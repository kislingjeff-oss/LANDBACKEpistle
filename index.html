An Epistle to FriendsRegarding Community, Mutual Aid and LANDBACK

In 2023, Jeff Kisling wrote a letter to Friends everywhere — about hunger in a rich country, about who that hunger falls hardest on, and about two practices, Mutual Aid and LANDBACK, he believes Quakers are being called toward.

JEFF KISLING · FIRST PUBLISHED OCTOBER 9, 2023
What's an epistle? In Quaker practice, an epistle is a formal letter one meeting — or one Friend — sends to encourage, challenge, or stay in unity with Friends elsewhere. Jeff modeled this one directly on a much older tradition: An Epistle to Friends Concerning Military Conscription, the kind of letter Quakers have sent each other for generations explaining why conscience won't let them cooperate with something. Here, what he's declining to cooperate with isn't a draft board. It's an economy.
I.

Why write this at all?

Jeff opens with a test, not a theory: a community's measure isn't what it believes, it's whether people's needs actually get met. By that test, he argues, something is badly wrong — not because resources are scarce, but because the system sorts who gets to eat.

"The measure of a community is how the needs of its people are met. No one should go hungry, or without shelter or healthcare. Yet in this country known as the United States millions struggle to survive."

— from the epistle's opening
II.

The diagnosis

Jeff's argument is blunt: a system that requires money for survival will always deny survival to whoever has none, no matter how much food is sitting in the store. He names directly — this isn't an accident of the system, it's who the system was built to serve, going back to stolen land and enslaved labor. communities carry the heaviest weight of it today.

He goes further than critique: he writes that he and other Friends to capitalism and — the same language, deliberately, that Quakers have used for generations to refuse military service.

And he doesn't make this case only in his own voice. He grounds it directly in words from people living the harm, not describing it from a distance.

In their own words

"Capitalism is about the value of people, property and the people who own property. Those who have wealth and property control the decisions that are made... In the name of liberation, capitalism must be reversed and dismantled."

— Des Moines Black Liberation Movement, quoted directly in the epistle

"Capitalism is revealed as an unjust, untenable system, when there is plenty of food in the grocery stores, but men, women and children are going hungry, living on the streets outside."

"It has become clear to some of us who are called Friends that the colonial capitalist economic system and white supremacy are contrary to the Spirit and we must find a better way. We conscientiously object to capitalism and white supremacy."

— from the epistle
III.

Mutual Aid

is Jeff's proposed alternative — and he's careful to define it narrowly. It isn't charity. It isn't a committee deciding what "those people" need. It's everyone, together, physically present with each other, solving a shared problem as equals. No one is only a giver; no one is only a receiver.

He names a second thing mutual aid does, beyond meeting needs: it changes the people doing it. Working side by side as equals, with no one "in charge," is itself practice for the kind of world without hierarchy that he says depends on.

Food giveaways

Often the first project a mutual aid network takes on — meeting the most immediate need.

Housing & shelter

Helping neighbors who need a place to stay — no application, no means test.

Bail funds

Posting bail for people arrested while organizing for change.

Court accompaniment

Showing up alongside people when they have to face the system — so no one goes alone.

"Mutual Aid involves everyone coming together to find a solution for problems we all face. This is a radical departure from 'us' helping 'them'. Instead, we all work together to find and implement solutions."

"With Mutual Aid, people learn to live in a community where there is no vertical hierarchy. A community where everyone has a voice."

— from the epistle
IV.

LANDBACK

If mutual aid answers "how do we meet needs without a hierarchy," answers a deeper question Jeff says has to come with it: whose land is this economy sitting on in the first place? He's careful to note it's about more than transferring deeds — it includes language, traditions, food sovereignty, housing, and clean air and water.

He cites organizer Nickita Longman, who frames LANDBACK broadly: almost any time Indigenous people push back against the state that displaced them, she argues, they're practicing some form of it already — not waiting for permission to begin. Again, Jeff doesn't just summarize this — he hands the sentence to her directly.

In their own words

"Any time an Indigenous person or nation has pushed back against the oppressive state, they are exercising some form of landback."

— Nickita Longman, community organizer, George Gordon First Nation, Saskatchewan — quoted directly in the epistle

"The other component necessary to move away from colonial capitalism and white supremacy is LANDBACK... The movement goes beyond the transfer of deeds to include respecting Indigenous rights, preserving languages and traditions, and ensuring food sovereignty, housing, and clean air and water."

— from the epistle, citing Claire Elise Thompson, "Returning the Land," Grist, Feb. 25, 2020
V.

What will Friends do?

Jeff turns, finally, from diagnosis to Friends themselves. His challenge is plain: it doesn't matter what a meeting says it believes if its daily collaboration with an unjust system tells a different story. Belief that doesn't show up in action, he writes, fails to speak to the world at all.

What Jeff is asking Friends to leave behind
  • Collaborating quietly with a system that leaves people hungry
  • Letting stated beliefs and daily life stay disconnected
  • Treating this as someone else's work to do
What he's asking Friends to build instead
  • Mutual aid and LANDBACK, lived out locally
  • , built by reaching out to neighbors
  • Changed lives first — the testimony follows

"Let our lives speak for our convictions."

Jeff Kisling
practicing hope
VI.

The Full Epistle

The letter in full, on one page, exactly as Jeff wrote it.

Dear Friends,

The measure of a community is how the needs of its people are met. No one should go hungry, or without shelter or healthcare. Yet in this country known as the United States millions struggle to survive. The capitalist economic system creates hunger, houselessness, illness that is preventable and despair. A system that requires money for goods and services denies basic needs to anyone who does not have money. Black, Indigenous, and other people of color (BIPOC) are disproportionately affected. Systemic racism. The capitalist system that supports the white materialistic lifestyle is built on stolen land and genocide of Indigenous peoples, and the labor of those who were enslaved in the past or are forced to live on poverty wages today.

Capitalism is revealed as an unjust, untenable system, when there is plenty of food in the grocery stores, but men, women and children are going hungry, living on the streets outside. White supremacy violently enforces the will of wealthy white people on the rest of us.

It has become clear to some of us who are called Friends that the colonial capitalist economic system and white supremacy are contrary to the Spirit and we must find a better way. We conscientiously object to capitalism and white supremacy.

capitalism has violated the communities of marginalized folks. capitalism is about the value of people, property and the people who own property. those who have wealth and property control the decisions that are made. the government comes second to capitalism when it comes to power.

in the name of liberation, capitalism must be reversed and dismantled. meaning that capitalistic practices must be reprogrammed with mutual aid practices.

— Des Moines Black Liberation Movement

Mutual Aid

What do we do instead? We rebuild our communities in ways not based upon money. Such communities thrive all over the world. Indigenous peoples have always lived this way. Generations of white people once did so in this country. Mutual Aid is a framework that can help us do this today.

The concept of Mutual Aid is simple to explain but can result in transformative change. Mutual Aid involves everyone coming together to find a solution for problems we all face. This is a radical departure from "us" helping "them". Instead, we all work together to find and implement solutions. To work together means we must be physically present with each other. Mutual Aid cannot be done by committee or donations. We build Beloved Community as we get to know each other. Build solidarity. An important part of Mutual Aid is creating these networks of people who know and trust each other. When new challenges arise, these networks are in place, ready to meet them.

Another important part of Mutual Aid is the transformation of those involved. This means both those who are providing help, and those receiving it.

With Mutual Aid, people learn to live in a community where there is no vertical hierarchy. A community where everyone has a voice. A model that results in enthusiastic participation. A model that makes the vertical hierarchy required for white supremacy impossible.

Commonly there are several Mutual Aid projects in a community. The initial projects usually relate to survival needs. One might be a food giveaway. Another helping those who need shelter. Many Mutual Aid groups often have a bail fund, to support those arrested for agitating for change. And accompany those arrested when they go to court.

LANDBACK

The other component necessary to move away from colonial capitalism and white supremacy is LANDBACK.

But the idea of "landback" — returning land to the stewardship of Indigenous peoples — has existed in different forms since colonial governments seized it in the first place. "Any time an Indigenous person or nation has pushed back against the oppressive state, they are exercising some form of landback," says Nickita Longman, a community organizer from George Gordon First Nation in Saskatchewan, Canada.

The movement goes beyond the transfer of deeds to include respecting Indigenous rights, preserving languages and traditions, and ensuring food sovereignty, housing, and clean air and water. Above all, it is a rallying cry for dismantling white supremacy and the harms of capitalism.

Source: Claire Elise Thompson, "Returning the Land: Four Indigenous Leaders Share Insights About the Growing Landback Movement and What It Means for the Planet," Grist, February 25, 2020.

What will Friends do?

It matters little what people say they believe when their actions are inconsistent with their words. Thus, we Friends may say there should not be hunger and poverty, but as long as Friends continue to collaborate in a system that leaves many without basic necessities and violently enforces white supremacy, our example will fail to speak to the world.

Let our lives speak for our convictions. Let our lives show that we oppose the capitalist system and white supremacy, and the damages that result. We can engage in efforts, such as Mutual Aid and LANDBACK, to build Beloved Community. To reach out to our neighbors to join us.

We must begin by changing our own lives if we hope to make a real testimony for peace and justice.

Practicing hope,

Jeff Kisling

Note: Modeled from "An Epistle to Friends Concerning Military Conscription." First published October 9, 2023.

VII.

The epistle it follows

Jeff's letter is written in the shape of a much older one: An Epistle to Friends Concerning Military Conscription, issued by a group of Friends drawn from several Yearly Meetings — including Iowa (Conservative) — and published through the Quaker peace-witness site Inward Light.

That epistle made an argument some Friends found startling: refusing to carry a weapon isn't enough. Accepting civilian alternative service, or even a conscientious-objector deferment, still means cooperating with a conscription system built to force people into war. The signers called on Friends to go further — to decline registration and induction outright, and to treat pacifism as something lived daily, not claimed only when a draft board asks.

It's that shape of refusal Jeff is borrowing: not "I won't personally take part in this harm," but "I won't cooperate with the system that produces it." In his letter, the system is a colonial capitalist economy rather than a draft board — but the structure of conscience, and even one of its lines, carry straight across.

For Jeff, the connection isn't just literary. Among its signers are his cousin, Roy Knight, and Don Laughlin, the Friend who mentored him in Quaker practice — and it was encountering this epistle itself, through that connection, that led him to write one of his own.

"We will risk being put in prison before we help turn men into murderers."

"Let our lives speak for our convictions."

— from "An Epistle to Friends Concerning Military Conscription" (that second line is the one Jeff echoes directly in Part V above)

The full text isn't reproduced here, since it belongs to its own signers and context — read it in full at inwardlight.org.

Queries for Reflection
